From Indian Spiritual Practice to Global Commodity

One everyday object, traced through culture, materials, value chains, people, and the environment — after Appadurai, The Social Life of Things (1986).

1Cultural Dimension — How a Practice Became a Product CULTURALQUALITATIVE

Indian spiritual practice

Yoga as philosophy, discipline and embodied tradition in South Asia

Transnational encounters

Modern posture practice reshaped through gymnastics, print culture & cross-border exchange (Singleton, 2010)

Global wellness boom

Stress relief, fitness & lifestyle — yoga spreads across Europe & North America

Commodity culture

Knowledge & practice converted into branded goods: mats, apparel, apps (Fish, 2006)

The yoga mat is not just equipment — it is material evidence of the globalization of yoga itself: a South Asian practice reimagined as a worldwide consumer activity, with the mat as its most iconic commodity.

5Social & Economic Actors — Who Touches the Mat? SOCIALECONOMIC

Ads frame the mat with balance, nature, awareness — "eco", "natural", "recyclable" claims sell products, but the EU requires environmental claims to be specific, accurate and verifiable across the whole life cycle (European Commission, n.d.).

Plastics damage the environment at every stage — production, use and disposal are connected problems that must be tackled together (UNEP, 2024). Multi-layer foams, prints and hidden additives make mats hard to recycle, so most end up as trash, are burned, or are down-cycled.

8Toward a Circular Model — Who Does What? QUALITATIVEECONOMIC
ConsumersBuy less, choose well, care for mats, repair, pass on — individual habits matter, but are not enough alone.
BrandsDurable mono-material designs, transparent chemical info, recycled inputs, credible green claims (EC, n.d.).
RetailersTake-back and return programmes, repair services, honest labelling across the whole life cycle.
ManufacturersReplaceable components, demonstrated recycling pathways, safer chemistry (UNEP, 2024).

Change is needed at both levels — consumer practice and producer responsibility — to move from a linear take–make–throw system to a circular one (EC First Circular Economy Action Plan, n.d.).
